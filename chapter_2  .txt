CHAPTER TWO: LITERATURE SURVEY

2.1 The Evolution of Intrusion Detection: From Human Observation to AI

Safeguarding infrastructure is an indispensable duty performed by Intrusion Detection Systems (IDS) within cybersecurity.early iterations of IDS heavily relied on manual human observation and primitive pattern identification to isolate network intrusions. However, manual triage has proven entirely ineffective when countering sophisticated, rapidly evolving categories of cyber threats.
As the digital landscape shifted, cybersecurity experts realized that the massive, accelerating adoption of cloud computing directly correlated with an expanded attack surface. This evolution created a critical demand for modern IDS platforms capable of identifying complex perimeter breaches and ensuring long-term data safety. Over time, researchers proposed diverse methodologies to identify and mitigate network attacks, progressively moving from rigid, signature-based rules to more flexible anomaly-based and hybrid detection structures.
Despite these iterative improvements, standard enterprise measures frequently struggle when deployed  dense cloud environments and high-speed network traffic. The processing  and strict rules of legacy setups cannot maintain real-time protection under extreme throughput. These explicit operational limitations ultimately triggered the development of Artificial Intelligence (AI)-based IDS architectures, which facilitate the rapid, automated, and effective analysis of network-layer threats. In recent years, a growing body of research has introduced varied Machine Learning (ML) and Deep Learning (DL) algorithms into the cybersecurity space, significantly augmenting the defensive capabilities of modern network perimeters.

2.2 Combining Unsupervised and Supervised Learning to Catch New Threats

To analyze high-throughput network traffic without introducing catastrophic processing latency, recent literature focus heavily on optimizing data ingestion. High-dimensional packet telemetry often contains redundant or non-contributory feature variables that bloat training intervals and degrade classifier precision. To remediate this bottleneck, Kostiuk et al. (2025) introduced the SentinelIDS framework, which prioritizes advanced feature-space dimensionality reduction. By implementing mathematical mapping techniques ,specifically Principal Component Analysis (PCA) and Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP). the researchers successfully compressed complex, raw network traffic feature vectors into dense, low-dimensional representations without cancel latent behavioral traits.
Beyond data optimization, the core innovation of the SentinelIDS framework lies in its branchs, two-stage hybrid processing pipeline, which systematically isolates zero-day network threats. Traditional signature-matching mechanisms remain fundamentally blind to novel, uncatalogued vulnerability exploits. Kostiuk et al. (2025) bypassed this limitation by structuring a multi-tiered logical pipeline:
·	Tier 1 (Unsupervised Profiling): An unsupervised learning layer combining an Autoencoder with an Isolation Forest maps and establishes an invariant statistical baseline of normal, healthy network traffic behavior.
·	Tier 2 (Supervised Classification): Any telemetry deviating from this baseline is flagged as anomalous and passed to a supervised Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost) classifier, which isolates and maps the anomaly to known multi-class attack categories.
When evaluated against modern, highly imbalanced network traffic benchmarks, this multi-stage pipeline demonstrated exceptional operational capability, achieving an overall classification accuracy of 99.87% while maintaining a remarkably low false alarm rate of 0.088%. This study proved that filtering raw traffic flows through sequential unsupervised and supervised layers allows an NIDS to flag previously unseen zero-day anomalies without bloating computational overhead.

2.3 Using Retraining to Fix Shifting Traffic Patterns Over Time

While two-stage static hybrid architectures achieve high immediate accuracy, their long-term operational efficacy is often stopped by a phenomenon known as "concept drift." In live enterprise environments, benign network traffic patterns are not static; seasonal software updates, changing user behaviors, and evolving enterprise protocols continuously alter the baseline data distribution. Over time, static models mistake these natural behavioral shifts for malicious actions, causing a gradual explosion in false positive rates.
Addressing this core operational vulnerability, Kostiuk et al. (2026) developed an advanced, follow-up optimization framework designed to maintain high classification stability across volatile, real-world network environments. Similar to their 2025 baseline study, the researchers utilized PCA and UMAP to condense high-dimensional feature vectors extracted from the standard CICIDS2017 and UNSW-NB15 traffic datasets. Their core classification engine also relied on a blended unsupervised/supervised stack, using an Autoencoder, an Isolation Forest, and a One-Class Support Vector Machine (SVM) to discover deviations before routing them to an XGBoost module for multi-class threat categorization.
The defining scientific contribution of the 2026 framework is the integration of an adaptive update mechanism driven by Markov Decision Processes (MDP). Instead of leaving the model static, the MDP layer continuously tracks the mathematical transition states of incoming telemetry. When the statistical properties of the benign traffic drift past a calculated threshold, the MDP automatically triggers a real-time, closed-loop model retraining sequence.
Testing validated that this adaptive framework maintains a 97% real-time classification accuracy even among shifting traffic baselines. By coupling unsupervised anomaly detection with automated reinforcement loops, the study demonstrated a resilient approach to safeguarding critical information infrastructures against zero-day exploits without requiring disruptive manual intervention.
